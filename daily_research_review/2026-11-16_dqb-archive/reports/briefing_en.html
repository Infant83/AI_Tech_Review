When a language model predicts its next token, the preceding context is represented by thousands of numbers. Routing part of that computation through today's quantum hardware requires a way to divide this wide representation among small circuits. Amoussou and colleagues split the 2,048-dimensional hidden representation of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 into 32 chunks, each processed by an eight-qubit circuit. Classical operations mix the features before the circuits and recombine their outputs. The number of heads absorbs the width of the host model.[1]

The authors trained this module with classical statevector simulation to replace eight consecutive blocks in a relatively insensitive middle section of the model. Its perplexity on WikiText-2 and C4 stayed within about 1% of the original model; perplexity measures how well the model assigns probability to the actual next token in text. On a 156-qubit Heron processor, the 32 heads were packed into groups of 14, 14 and four for limited token-generation runs. The full perplexity evaluations used simulation, while hardware supplied circuit-execution and output comparisons. One token's circuit submission occupied the processor for roughly 1.5 minutes.[1]

Component tests reveal more about the function being recovered. In a separate replacement of a sensitive single block, the full module had lower perplexity than a control removing both the circuits and their learned classical readout. The nonlinear transformation supplied something the constrained classical mixers could not recover alone. Yet entangler-off controls at two training seeds, and frozen-circuit controls at one seed, also beat the full module in perplexity. A classical multilayer perceptron with a similar parameter count led most perplexity comparisons. These results move the question from whether the circuit-containing module contributes to which nonlinear representation and training are needed.[1]

Our earlier review, “Designing the full cost of quantum computation,” discussed useful information lost when rich time-series features are compressed into a small quantum circuit.[2] The new design offers a partitioning route: each circuit transforms one token's features, while the remaining attention layers exchange information between tokens. Accommodating a wide representation and integrating context are shared across the quantum and classical parts. More heads can accept a wider input, while also increasing the circuits and measurements required at each token.[1]

I would like to test this division of work in molecular-property prediction. The same molecular representation could feed trainable circuits, fixed circuits and classical nonlinear models, comparing which molecular families and properties change in error. Including input preparation and output acquisition in a common time budget would help identify the functions a small module can usefully supply inside a larger model.
