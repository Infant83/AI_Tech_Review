An asset manager has updated its covariance estimate and is waiting for every asset weight before placing the next orders. Completing the job requires returning numbers that the order system can read. This 15-minute Daily Quantum Exercise uses a two-asset minimum-variance portfolio to examine the cost between the solution produced by the quantum linear-systems algorithm HHL and the output an operational workflow needs.

Try the problem first

Open page 1 of the problem PDF. The solutions begin on page 2. Modeling risk through covariance with weights that sum to one follows the portfolio-optimization setting explained in the CVX Short Course.[1] This exercise allows short positions and omits expected return and transaction costs. The firm, data, timings and tolerances are educational assumptions.

HHL prepares a normalized quantum state proportional to the solution vector. Estimating a few expectation values associated with that state and reading every component as a classical number require different amounts of measurement.[2] Write down the state amplitudes, measurement probabilities and budget-normalized portfolio weights separately. Also consider how adding a diagonal term to improve conditioning changes the investment model itself.

For a research or operational study, start with a small covariance model and compare the classical solution with the weights reconstructed from the quantum state. Decide whether the task needs every order weight or only a particular risk statistic, then compare methods that return the same output. Recording allocation errors alongside latency, including input preparation, measurement and failure recovery, helps identify which stage a subsequent study should improve. This is a proposed validation approach for the hypothetical case.

Expand solutions and insights

Read amplitudes and probabilities from the same solution

Solving Ax=(1,1)ᵀ for A=[[4,1],[1,2]] gives x=(1/7,3/7)ᵀ. Normalizing by the component sum yields optimal weights w=(1/4,3/4)ᵀ. Daily variance is 1.75 (percentage points)², 12.5% below the equal-weight value of 2.00 (percentage points)². In fractional-return units it is 1.75×10⁻⁴. These are in-model comparisons for the specified covariance.

The ideal solution state is (|0⟩+3|1⟩)/√10, with measurement probabilities (0.1,0.9). Using those probabilities directly as weights gives variance 1.84 (percentage points)². Here both components are known to be positive, so taking square roots and normalizing their sum recovers the weights. A general real solution allowing short positions requires additional measurements to recover relative signs. The weights in this task are independent of the solution's overall scale; other outputs that use absolute magnitudes also require its norm.

Compare the easier model under matching conditions

Replacing A with A+I gives weights (1/3,2/3)ᵀ and variance 16/9≈1.7778 (percentage points)² under the original covariance. This exceeds the original minimum by 1/36, about 1.59%. The condition number falls from about 2.78 to 2.09, while the objective gains a ‖w‖² penalty on weight concentration. Compare solvers using the same A+I for the classical baseline and report risk under the original A as well. If preconditioning preserves the original solution, include the cost of constructing and accessing it and transforming the solution back.

Time completion of the full output

The assumed stages add up to 20+10+5+30+10=75 seconds, exceeding the 60-second target. The existing 90 seconds is a 95th-percentile latency; 75 seconds is an unvalidated single-run estimate for a future fault-tolerant device. Interpreting their simple ratio of 1.2 as a measured speedup would require latency distributions under matching inputs, accuracy and output requirements.

Use Cholesky factorization and triangular solves as a small positive-definite baseline; consider factor covariance models and preconditioned iterative methods at larger scales. The quantum estimate must include an oracle for accessing the dense covariance matrix, input-state preparation, conditioning, precision, success probability, repeated readout and error-correction resources. Cai and colleagues demonstrated a simple 2×2 linear-system instance with four photon qubits and four controlled logic gates.[3] That physical experiment and the exercise's 10,000-asset, 75-second future estimate have different scales and execution conditions.

Under the stated estimate, operational deployment is a No-Go, while a small-model validation study is a Pivot. The exercise's illustrative Go criteria require all of the following: 95th-percentile total latency ≤60 seconds, absolute error in each weight ≤0.005, budget residual |∑w−1|≤10⁻⁶ and excess variance above the optimum ≤1%. A weight error of 0.005 means 0.5 percentage points of allocation. Count failures and retries, and separately evaluate the estimated covariance model on subsequent data. These metrics are educational targets for an adoption study.
