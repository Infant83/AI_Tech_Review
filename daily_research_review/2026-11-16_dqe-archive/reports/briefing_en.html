When only one catalyst can enter the next reactor experiment, a small computed energy difference can determine what the team tests. This Daily Quantum Exercise is a 15-minute fictional comparison of catalysts A and B: correct their reaction barriers, then ask whether the difference survives the calculation's errors. It connects greater precision with the need to return a reliable recommendation on time.

Work through reactant and transition-state energy corrections, the error budget for ranking candidates, and the measurement cost of quantum calculations. The candidates, numbers, and 45-minute target are educational assumptions. One reaction step controls the rate, with equal prefactors and thermal and entropic corrections for the two candidates.

Using calculations to narrow experimental choices has a long research history. Jacobsen and colleagues studied the use of interpolation across the periodic table to design bimetallic ammonia-synthesis catalysts.[1] This exercise isolates a smaller decision: whether the computed energy difference at specified structures is clear enough to choose between two candidates.

A reaction barrier is the energy difference between a reactant and its transition state. The exercise uses CI-NEB to locate a saddle point along a path joining nuclear configurations, following the method introduced by Henkelman and colleagues.[2] Distinguishing the search for those structures from the electronic calculation at a fixed structure helps identify which errors and costs affect the recommendation.

A question to bring to experimental planning

The exercise offers a way to prioritize accuracy. Expressing the difference between candidates and the model and solver errors in the same units makes it possible to ask which uncertainty an additional calculation reduces. A catalyst-planning study could assemble activity data under matched conditions and independent validation cases, then test the stability of its recommendations. A different molecular or surface model needs its own accuracy requirements. These are proposed uses to explore beyond the fictional problem.

Separate a corrected ranking from a guaranteed ranking

Add δE(TS)−δE(R) to the DFT barrier. The corrected barriers are 0.76 eV for A and 0.67 eV for B, reversing the nominal choice from A to B. Under the exercise's equal-prefactor, single-rate-controlling-step assumptions, k(B)/k(A)=exp[(0.76−0.67)/0.050]≈6.05. Measured initial activity also depends on coverage, active-site density, and other reaction steps.

Adding the model and solver bounds gives a maximum barrier error of 0.060 eV per candidate. The difference between candidates can shift by as much as 0.120 eV, exceeding the nominal 0.090 eV gap. The supplied bounds therefore do not guarantee the ranking. With a solver error of 0.020 eV per candidate, a common model-error bound m must satisfy 0.090−2(m+0.020)>0, or m<0.025 eV. These errors already refer to barriers after taking energy differences. Convergence as the model grows is a diagnostic; an absolute error bound needs an independent reference or a validated error estimate.

Change the measurement budget together with accuracy

The proposed role for VQE is to obtain the lowest electronic energy in the specified charge and spin sector at each of four fixed structures, while retaining classical path finding. A transition state is a saddle point in nuclear coordinates; it does not by itself require an electronic excited-state calculation. Eight spatial orbitals correspond to 16 spin orbitals and, under the specified mapping, 16 data qubits per structure. Hempel and colleagues used up to four qubits on a trapped-ion device to calculate the ground-state energies of H₂ and LiH and investigate measurement noise and mitigation.[3] The 16-qubit catalyst model and timing here are separate educational settings.

The total is 4×100×50×500=10 million shots. At 5,000 shots/s, measurement takes 33 minutes 20 seconds; the other stages add 25 minutes, giving 58 minutes 20 seconds. Meeting 45 minutes leaves room for 6 million shots, a 40% reduction. With unchanged evaluation and group counts, that is an average of 300 shots per group. The reduced budget must be tested again against the 0.020 eV per-candidate barrier solver target, so meeting the schedule alone does not establish recommendation accuracy.

What could change the next decision

Compare solvers using FCI for the same small active-space Hamiltonian, integrals, geometry, charge, and spin; larger spaces can use a converged reference such as DMRG. Model checks can enlarge the active space and embedding and inspect the saddle point on the corrected energy surface for representative candidates. Independent activity data test the actual recommendation hit rate. Separating these comparisons shows what improvements in the electronic solver and the chemical model each contribute.

Under the present estimates, a reasonable decision is No-Go for operational use and Pivot to a small validation proof of concept. Illustrative Go targets are a 95th-percentile total turnaround within 45 minutes on a prespecified test set, solver error within 0.020 eV per candidate, a model-error bound below 0.025 eV for this example, and recommendation accuracy at least as high as the classical method on independent cases. Time both methods from preparation through completed validation on the same candidates, include failures, mitigation, and retries, and report paired confidence intervals for the hit-rate difference. These are proposed test targets. If an improved classical method meets them and the quantum calculation provides no demonstrated additional benefit, adopt the classical method.

Our Qunova HI-VQE review explains the division between a quantum device proposing electronic configurations and a classical subspace calculation obtaining energies. It compares active-space accuracy with total runtime. Its algorithm differs from the standard VQE used in this exercise, while offering a useful way to read which model was solved and where the cost of obtaining the result arose.
