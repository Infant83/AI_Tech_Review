Suppose a model reproduces how a material's magnetization changes under an applied magnetic field. Can the same spin interactions also explain its heat capacity as temperature changes? The α-RuCl₃ study that opens the November 13 DRB shows how important differences can remain among models that fit magnetization well. Choosing what to calculate leads directly to testing how much of the material that calculation explains.

Misawa and colleagues used Bayesian optimization to search five parameters against 36 magnetization data points measured along two directions at 1.4 K. Each candidate's magnetization comes from classical exact diagonalization of a 24-spin model. Yet even the region with a small magnetization error did not sufficiently determine the absolute magnitude of the Kitaev coupling K. Examining heat capacity while varying K reveals differences that magnetization alone leaves unresolved. A good fit becomes a starting point for candidate models to test with another observable.[1]

Once an input model is chosen, its quantum state still has to be found. Geier and Fu used neural-network variational Monte Carlo to solve a continuum moiré Hamiltonian with DFT-derived parameters. Their WSe₂/WS₂ ferromagnetic example contains 28 electrons in 21 unit cells. A central feature is diagnosing total spin while calculating in the S_z=0 sector. The first study infers interaction parameters from experiment; this one finds magnetic order under specified interactions. Reading spin correlations alongside energy reveals the character of the resulting state.[2]

For molecules at several geometries, energy differences and state ordering matter alongside the energy of any one state. Transferable DeepQMC, developed by Schätzle and colleagues, shares wavefunction parameters across geometries and electronic states and orders the states dynamically. In the 14-geometry setting for two ethylene singlets, the authors report a mean absolute relative-energy error of 4.7(7) meV against NES-VMC, after alignment by one common constant shift. That metric identifies the relationship between molecular structures being reproduced. Comparing joint optimization with separate calculations at each geometry likewise requires a shared relative-energy criterion and an accounting of computational cost.[3]

Our earlier LUSH review discussed learning a Hamiltonian that couples excited states to represent regions where their characters mix smoothly.[4] DeepQMC jointly optimizes wavefunctions through the variational principle in a classical calculation, while continuing the question of whether relations between states remain reliable as geometry changes. The earlier article on sparse references and quantum measurements showed how reading the desired energy difference still carries measurement costs and bias after a state has been prepared.[5] In the next comparison, I would first separate observables used to fit a model from those used to test it. Comparing optimization and measurement costs for the same model, physical quantity and target error would then make it clearer what understanding a larger calculation actually adds.
