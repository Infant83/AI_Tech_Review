An order changes just before the delivery vehicles leave. The dispatcher has little time to choose a new plan. A short route is usable only if the combined assignment serves every order once. This Daily Quantum Exercise turns that situation into a fictional distribution center and a 15-minute problem: selecting routes while returning a usable plan before the deadline.

Try the problem first

Open page 1 of the problem PDF. Solutions begin on page 2. Work through a four-order, two-vehicle example, formulate the route selection as an integer program and a QUBO, and design a quantum hybrid experiment with a 30-second deadline. If routing is unfamiliar, start by checking whether each order appears in exactly one selected route.

Real delivery planning combines travel times, permitted visit windows, and vehicle capacities. The official Google OR-Tools example shows how cumulative arrival-time variables implement time-window constraints.[1] The small exercise assumes each individual route has already passed its checks, allowing attention to focus on missing or duplicated orders across routes. Selecting from candidate routes can be formulated as set partitioning, a connection to classical work that adds routes while solving time-window routing problems.[2]

Bringing the question to another task

The exercise helps translate an operational request into conditions a solver can evaluate. Expressing a good dispatch plan in terms of distance, assignments per order, vehicle count, and a return deadline makes the comparison concrete. A role for the quantum device also requires specifying its inputs and expected outputs, together with the time available when it is connected to the other stages.

The same reasoning could inform field-service visit schedules or the selection of manufacturing work bundles. First specify the work and resources represented by each candidate and the combinations that cannot be selected together. Each application requires its own time, staff, and equipment constraints. These are proposed extensions of the exercise to explore.

When two good routes form a usable dispatch plan

The feasible assignments are R1+R2 at 87 km, R3+R4 at 81 km, and R5+R6 at 76 km. The last saves 11 km, approximately 12.6%, relative to the initial plan. R3+R5 totals 73 km but serves A twice and omits B. Both distance and feasibility determine the best dispatch plan. The 12.6% saving belongs to this educational example and its initial-plan baseline.

A QUBO is an objective containing linear and quadratic terms in binary variables. Here it adds squared penalties for missing or duplicated orders to the sum of route distances. With a penalty coefficient λ=88 km, an infeasible assignment has energy at least 88 km, while the feasible initial plan costs 87 km. This is sufficient to make the global optimum feasible. On hardware, rescaling coefficients into the permitted range can make distance differences harder to resolve, so returned samples still require constraint checks. All 64 assignments of the six-variable example were independently enumerated on a classical computer.

Which part must change to meet 30 seconds?

The sequential estimate allocates 8 seconds to input preparation, 6 to embedding, 18 to waiting and communication, 2 to the device stage, and 6 to reconstruction and validation: 40 seconds in total. Reusing the embedding leaves 34 seconds. Even with a two-second device stage, meeting the deadline requires changes to the surrounding stages. These are stipulated estimates, rather than measured 95th-percentile operational latencies.

A hardware routing study by Feld and colleagues illustrates why the timing boundary matters. For five subproblems combined, the authors reported 0.11 seconds of QPU access time and 0.234 seconds for local classical QBSolv. They also recorded that the local approach was faster in total computation time when overheads including embedding were counted. These results appear in Section 5.2.3 and Table 4 of that benchmark.[3]

What the next experiment could establish

One possible design keeps unaffected routes fixed and tests route selection within a small affected area. Replay paired order-change records with identical candidate routes, fixed routes, and vehicle limits, comparing a time-limited integer program with the quantum hybrid. Compare against plans using the full candidate pool as well, to measure the loss in plan quality caused by restricting that area. Solving the same QUBO classically also helps distinguish encoding problems from device sampling quality. Evaluate the original order constraints, kilometers, and time from input preparation to completed validation. Include failures and timeouts, using the distance of the operational fallback plan in those cases.

Under these fictional conditions, a reasonable decision is to postpone real-time deployment and pivot to a small-area proof of concept. The exercise offers illustrative acceptance targets: a validated plan returned within 30 seconds in at least 95% of all trials, zero constraint violations in dispatched plans, and at least a 2% mean distance saving against the classical baseline on the same cases. These are proposed operational targets, not demonstrated results. A classical improvement that meets the targets should also inform the adoption decision.

Our related review, Beyond qubit counts: quantum computing becomes a systems competition, follows the resources and recovery costs involved in turning device samples into valid solutions. This routing exercise makes that perspective something to calculate and turn into an experiment.
