Two calculations of a P450 electronic-structure model met their energy-convergence criteria yet ended about 31.8 millihartrees (mHa) apart. Koh and colleagues revisit this case in a new preprint, using an active space of 25 electrons and 23 orbitals. Changes to initialization and orbital ordering led density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) calculations to a lower-energy solution under the same Hamiltonian and spin-sector constraints. That energy agreed with an independently optimized selected configuration interaction (SCI) result. Small energy changes during optimization had not identified which state the calculation reached.[1]

This matters for quantum cost estimates. Attributing a high-energy plateau solely to insufficient resources can make an optimization difficulty look like intrinsic classical hardness. Transferring that solution into quantum phase estimation (QPE) also requires checking its overlap with the desired lower-energy state. The preparation and repetition costs discussed in our earlier full-workflow review therefore depend on the reliability of the reference state.[1, 2]

The paper's separate cost comparison favors classical computation for six of seven representative systems, assuming US$0.04125 per CPU core-hour and US$7,000 per whole future fault-tolerant QPU-hour. The classical 1.6 mHa perturbative-correction threshold is a convergence diagnostic; the quantum 1 mHa target is phase-estimation precision. They are not identical guarantees of total energy error. FeMoco's US$721–12,114 break-even hourly range reflects alternative classical extrapolations. Applying such estimates to a research budget requires recording how the target state and accuracy were established.[1]
