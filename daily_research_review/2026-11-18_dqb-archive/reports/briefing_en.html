Given a polynomial, counting how many of several thousand conditions it satisfies can be easier than finding a high-scoring polynomial in the first place. Optimal Polynomial Intersection (OPI) uses that difference to test quantum computation: a quantum processor supplies a candidate, and a classical computer checks its score. Checking the score does not establish that the candidate is globally optimal.

Khattar and colleagues' paper on decoded quantum interferometry (DQI), accepted by Physical Review X on November 17, turns this search into a concrete circuit budget. A representative instance requires 1,885 logical qubits and approximately 5.72 million Toffoli gates per shot. The authors reduce reversible Reed–Solomon decoding costs by changing how intermediate information is represented and sharing registers. The preprint estimate of more than 10^23 classical trials applies to the Extended Prange attack model they analyze.[1]

The next step is to connect a circuit execution with a successful answer. Error correction can protect an execution while the probability of sampling the target score remains a separate issue. The November 17 code release distinguishes weights that maximize the finite-size mean score from weights that maximize the probability of exceeding a target.[2] A total runtime therefore needs both an error-corrected architecture and the repetitions required by that output criterion.

Aqarios encounters a related question on actual IBM Heron hardware. Its constrained optimizer returns high-quality feasible solutions using 74–132 qubits, with variable fixing before sampling and classical processing afterward. IBM notes that classical and quantum samples can reach similar final quality after equivalent postprocessing.[3] The relevant question is what each sampler contributes to the solution ultimately returned.

Our earlier review, Designing the full cost of quantum computation, examines space, waiting and repetition costs.[4] DQI makes that discussion concrete for a search whose outputs can be checked. Defining the answer we need also identifies what must be counted between one circuit execution and a completed task.
