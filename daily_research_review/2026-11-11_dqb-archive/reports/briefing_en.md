Suppose an optimization algorithm improves the average score of its candidates. We still need a usable solution, and the average does not tell us how often one appears. Several studies in the November 11 DQB make this gap tangible. Connecting a better quantum output to a better final result requires following both the output distribution and the next stage that consumes it.

Fahim and Hossain reused QAOA angles learned on a small training set and ran the circuits on 12 physical qubits of IBM Heron. In the main test family, hardware retained 29–46% of the noiseless improvement in mean gap over uniform sampling, but only 5–15% of the improvement in optimum probability. The former averages problem-level retention ratios within each run; the latter takes their median. Both subtract the uniform contribution. The 5–15% figure is therefore a retention ratio, rather than the raw probability of finding the optimum.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

The distribution explains the mismatch. A post hoc analysis found that hardware preserved the ability to avoid poor candidates better than its concentration on optimal and near-optimal ones.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span> The average can improve while useful candidates remain scarce. Our earlier review, The Boundary of Quantum Advantage Keeps Moving<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-2">2</a>]</span>, argued for recording the observable, cost denominator, and execution boundary together. These measured distributions show why choosing the observable is consequential.

Even frequent good subproblem solutions need not improve the full algorithm. The same researchers tested QAOA inside ADMM, which alternates updates of binary and continuous variables. On a separate planted general family, noiseless QAOA solved 97.8% of subproblems exactly, but reached the full optimum on 77.5% of instances. Four classical local heuristics, despite lower subproblem accuracy, reached 92.5–96.2%. This comparison concerns the tested two-block ADMM with its penalty fixed at ρ=1. Accurate subproblem updates could remain stuck, while variability from approximate solutions could move the iteration away.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-3">3</a>]</span>

Forecasting also requires choosing the unit of success. Connerty and colleagues fed compressed flow and reaction–diffusion data into a fixed quantum reservoir, then trained a classical linear predictor on its measured features. On IBM hardware with 26 or 52 qubits, normalized RMSE (NRMSE) for one-step forecasts and pressure estimation was 21.2–41.1% below a linear baseline using the same compressed representation. Readout settings were selected by test error, so an independent evaluation remains necessary. The timing table compares device execution with the sum of GPU work used to simulate those circuits exactly. Finishing the full forecasting task faster than a classical PDE solver requires a different experiment.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-4">4</a>]</span>

Reading this beside the earlier Korean-language review of quantum-informed chaotic forecasting<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-5">5</a>]</span> suggests a useful comparison. Its QIML design learns a statistical quantum prior offline and passes it to a classical predictor. The new reservoir acquires quantum measurement features during forecasting. The designs therefore pay their quantum costs at different stages. On a shared task, we could compare short-horizon error, preservation of long-time distributions and energy spectra, and the repeated measurements needed to achieve that quality. Their published numbers come from different systems and tests and cannot establish a ranking.

The research choice I would make first is to define a successful output. For optimization, that means an objective tolerance and satisfied constraints; for forecasting, a required horizon and physical quantities to preserve. We can then count measurement and classical computation until that target is reached. Locating where an intermediate improvement survives or disappears helps identify whether the next change belongs in the circuit, the measurement, or the full algorithm.
