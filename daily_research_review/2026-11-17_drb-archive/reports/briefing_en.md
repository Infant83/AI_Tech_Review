Connecting quantum processors with photons requires the sender and receiver to perform compatible operations. Even photons in the same frequency band can produce different reception outcomes when their temporal amplitudes and phases differ. Hernández-Antón and colleagues used this temporal structure to select which photons a superconducting receiver would absorb.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

The team transmitted single photons in three orthogonal temporal modes, one mode per experiment, across a 30 m cryogenic link. For the real waveforms used here, orthogonality means that positive and negative contributions cancel when two different waveforms are multiplied and integrated over time. With a matching reception pulse, the receiving transmon's |f⟩-state population was 75–79%; with a different mode, it was 1–4%. The reported selectivity of about 40 is the mean matched-mode population divided by the mean mismatched-mode population. The receiver could thus choose which temporal mode to absorb and which to reflect.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

An optical quantum-dot experiment controlled emission direction through interference. Dots grown in a semiconductor vary in size and therefore in emission frequency. Henke and colleagues independently tuned two dots, separated by 13 µm, into resonance using local electrodes and coupled them through a shared waveguide. Changing the relative phase of the two optical drives changed the balance between the left and right outputs. The waveguide itself supports both directions; interference between the two emission amplitudes favors one output. The measured directional balance depends on the collection window, with the paper reporting 0.4 ns and 3 ns windows separately.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-2">2</a>]</span>

An APS Physics Focus highlighted the realization of directional control with optical quantum dots following earlier microwave demonstrations. External researchers Yasunobu Nakamura of the University of Tokyo and Stuart Masson of the University of South Florida discussed its relevance to optical quantum networks and controllable quantum-light sources.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-3">3</a>]</span>

The two experiments control different parts of a quantum connection. In the cryogenic microwave link, waveform overlap determines which photon the receiver absorbs. In the optical waveguide, interference between two emitters determines the output balance. A subsequent review could examine these controls alongside loss, timing alignment and phase stability to explain what must be designed and measured when connecting quantum modules.
