A quantum processor can supply candidate electronic configurations; a classical computer then solves for a molecular energy in the space they span. In large calculations, researchers also use extrapolation to estimate a more precise energy from finite results. Qunova’s iron–sulfur announcement, revisited in the 15 November DQB, becomes more interesting when we follow those two energies separately. On 12 November, the company reported an 80-qubit HI-VQE calculation using IBM Quantum System Two at RIKEN together with the Fugaku supercomputer.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

Its headline value is −327.2482 ± 0.0007 hartree. The company reports this as an extrapolated estimate; it does not publish the last directly calculated subspace energy. The central value differs by 1.1 millihartree from its DMRG reference of −327.2471 hartree, within the conventional chemical-accuracy scale of about 1.6 millihartree. The DMRG reference is itself an extrapolation to infinite bond dimension, the parameter controlling its retained calculation space. The close agreement is between two extrapolated estimates.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-2">2</a>]</span> The computed points, fit function and meaning of the ±0.7 millihartree uncertainty would show more precisely how that accuracy was obtained.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

The reported cost reduction belongs to an intermediate result. Qunova says that its second iteration reached −326.9821 hartree using 2,518 Fugaku node-hours, claiming an energy below an earlier SQD result at roughly 300 times lower cost. This figure does not give the accumulated cost through the final extrapolated result. I would like to see the energy-convergence curve paired with the quantum and classical resources accumulated at each point. That would support a comparison of total cost at matched accuracy.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

Our earlier review, “Two computers calculating a molecule: Where does quantum advantage come from?”, explained HI-VQE’s division of work: quantum hardware proposes configurations and classical computation solves the wavefunction in a selected space.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-3">3</a>]</span> The paper reviewed then assigns 72 problem qubits plus eight connectors to its iron–sulfur calculation. The number 80 in the new announcement therefore does not establish a larger active space. The public revision does not supply the announcement’s final extrapolated value or fitting procedure.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-4">4</a>]</span> The useful continuation is to examine how candidate discovery, space expansion and extrapolation each contribute to accuracy. Yesterday’s briefing discussed a learned circuit generator aimed at improving the candidates supplied in the first stage.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-5">5</a>]</span> Holding classical processing and the total budget fixed, I would compare candidate generators and track both directly computed and extrapolated energies.
