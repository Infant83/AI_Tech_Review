When two machines share one maintenance crew and one crane, task order and resource handovers determine how long the shutdown lasts. This 15-minute Daily Quantum Exercise asks you to prove the optimality of a small maintenance schedule, then calculate the probability that quantum annealing returns a suitable schedule within a computation budget. It connects schedule quality with the reliability of the computation used to obtain it.

Try the problem first

Open page 1 of the problem PDF. Solutions begin on page 2. Draw the four task intervals and find a reason why a shorter schedule is impossible. Then encode start times as binary variables and form a QUBO that penalizes constraint violations. The OR-Tools job-shop example models the same kinds of precedence and exclusive-resource constraints.[1]

Keep the small hand-calculation case separate from the operational-scale execution estimate. Maintenance completion is measured in hours; the elapsed time used to compute a new schedule after an input change is measured in seconds. The factory, durations, success probabilities and solver timings are educational assumptions. The repeated-sampling formula specifically assumes independent samples with a constant success probability.

For a real maintenance study, specify task precedence, shared resources, travel and setup times from data, and set a separate deadline for returning a new schedule. Compare a classical solver that improves an existing feasible schedule with a quantum sampler under matching constraints. Record the fraction of validated schedules returned on time alongside total latency. This is a proposed follow-up study; the toy optimum and improvements at an actual factory require separate evidence.

Expand solutions and insights

Match a constructed schedule to a lower bound

Run A1 and B1 on the crew and crane respectively during [0,2), then exchange resources for A2 and B2 during [2,5). The makespan is 5 hours. Each machine's sequential work and each resource's total load also take 5 hours, giving a matching lower bound. The constructed schedule therefore proves optimality. A half-open interval [s,e) allows the next task to start exactly when the previous task ends.

For a deadline D=5 hours, let xᵢₜ indicate whether task i starts at allowed time t. Add a one-start term (∑ₜxᵢₜ−1)², products for start pairs that violate precedence, and products for overlapping tasks on the same resource. Count each overlap pair once. All penalties are nonnegative, so with positive coefficients in this feasibility-only problem, H=0 corresponds exactly to a feasible schedule. Each 2-hour task has four candidate starts and each 3-hour task has three, giving 14 binary variables before pruning. This is a logical variable count; physical qubits depend on the embedding graph and chains.

Connect single-sample success to the return deadline

For independent samples with constant success probability p, the probability of at least one success among n samples is 1−(1−p)ⁿ. The minimum counts for 95% success are 29 at p=0.10 and 149 at p=0.02. Adding the fixed 8-second overhead once gives total times of 9.45 and 15.45 seconds. The first meets the 10-second target, while the classical baseline needs 2 seconds for the same operational instance, deadline and 95% success criterion. Under these assumptions, use the classical method operationally and restrict the quantum route to a validation proof of concept.

The 10-second budget leaves room for 40 samples after overhead. At p=0.02, their success probability is about 55.4%. The mean attempt count 1/p differs from the count required for a specified success level. In an actual study, estimate confidence intervals for success probabilities, inspect sample autocorrelation and measure batch success across dates, embeddings and instances. Dependence or changing success probabilities requires revisiting the formula's assumptions.

Separate a search outcome from an infeasibility proof

Failing to sample a schedule that meets deadline D is a search failure. An infeasibility claim needs evidence such as a proven lower bound above D or a certificate from an exact solver. Record the classical CP-SAT incumbent, lower bound and termination status, and solve the same QUBO classically to check the encoding. Include preparation, embedding, chain repair and sample validation in timing and constraint-violation records.

Venturelli and colleagues studied job-shop instances with up to six jobs and six machines on the D-Wave Vesuvius processor using a time-indexed QUBO, pruning and embedding.[2] This was an author-reported physical-QPU study; the exercise's probabilities and second-scale timings are separate assumptions. A follow-up adoption test could require the lower endpoint of a 95% confidence interval for returning valid, deadline-compliant schedules within 10 seconds to reach 95%, together with a time or quality improvement over the matching classical baseline. These are validation targets proposed in the exercise.

The earlier D-Wave molecular inverse-design experiment compared exact search, classical simulated annealing and a QPU on the same QUBO, checking sample cardinality constraints and broken embedding chains separately. Comparing that case with this schedule shows what to record when validating the encoding, sample feasibility, hardware embedding and computational time.
