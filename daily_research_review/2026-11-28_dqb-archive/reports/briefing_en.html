A candidate answer to a 10,000-variable binary problem sits in classical memory. In Quantum Subspace Sampling Descent (QSSD), proposed by Alonso and colleagues, only a subset of its variables is selected for quantum sampling. The others retain their current values, while their interactions with the selected variables enter the coefficients of a smaller Hamiltonian. The algorithm revises part of an answer while retaining those connections to the rest.[1]

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) circuit angles are calculated in advance from each reduced problem's coefficients. A classical computer then improves measured candidates through local bit flips across the full set of variables. The resulting low-cost population also helps select the variables for the next iteration. This makes the sampler's supply of multiple candidates relevant to the search, beyond the quality of a single returned answer.[1]

On a 10,000-variable heavy-hex instance, 100 iterations of a 152-qubit, eight-layer circuit reached a solution-quality score of approximately 0.95, normalized using the minimum and maximum energies. It is not a percentage of correctly assigned variables. This was one complete hardware-based QSSD trial of 100 iterations, with 10,000 shots per circuit. On the same problem, simulated annealing reached 0.992, and HiGHS proved the optimum in 3,310 seconds. The structure of this instance therefore matters alongside its variable count when assessing classical difficulty.[1]

The earlier review, “The Boundary of Quantum Advantage Keeps Moving,” proposed comparing strong classical methods for the same target and replacing a hybrid calculation's quantum component to identify its contribution. QSSD makes that comparison concrete: keep the variable-selection and bit-flip rules unchanged, and change the sampler. A manufacturing-scheduling application could also measure how often feasible solutions are obtained within the same time budget. These matched-budget tests remain a proposal, separate from the paper's scores obtained under different execution budgets.[1, 2]
