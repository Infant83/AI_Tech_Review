Generation planning combines decisions about which units to turn on with decisions about how much power they supply. Commitment is binary, output is continuous, and minimum and maximum output limits connect them. This 15-minute Daily Quantum Exercise uses two generators over two hours to examine an approximate solution under the original operating constraints.

## Try the problem first

[Open page 1 of the problem PDF](https://infant83.io/AI_Tech_Review/archive/files/2026-11-22-daily-quantum-exercise.pdf#page=1). Solutions begin on page 2. First filter the feasible commitment combinations for each period, then calculate dispatch and cost. The PyPSA unit-commitment documentation explains how operating status, output bounds and startup costs are linked in a mixed-integer linear program (MILP).<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

The firm, generators, demand, costs and timings are educational assumptions. Each period lasts one hour; multiplying power in MW by period length gives the MWh used to calculate variable costs. Storage, curtailment, unserved demand and external trading are excluded. Ramping, reserves and minimum up-times are also omitted from this small case. Changing those conditions changes the set of feasible plans.

In a research or operational comparison, validate the quantum component's proposed commitments using the same original dispatch model, and record costs of feasible plans alongside failures and timeouts. A follow-up study could add demand forecasts or storage while giving both methods matching inputs and constraints and timing everything from preparation through validation. This is a proposed test approach; the exercise's numbers are not measured power-system operating results.

<h3>Calculate cost for feasible commitments</h3>
<p>Only B can supply the first period's 30 MW. A alone has a 40 MW minimum, while the two units together have a 50 MW minimum. The second period's 90 MW exceeds either unit's individual capacity, so both must run. Use the cheaper A up to 80 MW and let B supply 10 MW.</p>
<p>B starts in the first period and stays on; A starts in the second. Variable costs total 1,050+1,600+350=$3,000 and startup costs total 20+200=$220, giving an optimal total of $3,220. The approximate plan instead violates A's minimum-output constraint by assigning it 30 MW in the first period. Restoring startup costs to its $2,550 variable cost gives $2,770. The $450 difference cannot be reported as a realizable saving. Restoring the original constraints requires solving the commitment decisions again.</p>
<h3>Connect binary commitment to continuous dispatch</h3>
<p>Let binary variables uᵢₜ and vᵢₜ denote operating status and startup respectively, and let pᵢₜ be continuous output in MW. Starting from uᵢ₀=0, enforce supply-demand balance and status-dependent output bounds, and sum variable costs cᵢpᵢₜΔt and startup costs Sᵢvᵢₜ. With vᵢₜ≥uᵢₜ−u(i,t−1) and positive startup costs, v equals one only for actual 0→1 transitions at an optimum. This optimality-based equivalence differs from constraints that force the indicator to equal the transition for every feasible assignment.</p>
<p>For this exercise, QAOA could sample commitment candidates and a classical linear program could validate dispatch for each candidate under the original constraints. Two generators times two periods gives four binary commitment variables. Output discretization, auxiliary variables, physical connectivity, circuit depth and measurement costs need separate accounting. The cost obtained after classical dispatch optimization is not generally an exact quadratic function of u, so an implemented QAOA cost operator or fixed QUBO needs its own construction and justification.</p>
<p>Koretsky and colleagues simulated a unit-commitment method combining QAOA with a classical minimizer in Qiskit.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-2">2</a>]</span> Their outer classical optimization jointly adjusts continuous output, slack variables and QAOA parameters. Exact per-commitment dispatch validation in this exercise is a separate educational construction inspired by that division of work. Neither the paper nor this hand calculation establishes a timing advantage on an actual QPU.</p>
<h3>Time the complete route to a plan of matching quality</h3>
<p>The assumed sequential total is 8+40×0.30+5+15+4=44 seconds. It exceeds the 30-second target by 14 seconds and is slower than the assumed 15-second classical MILP baseline meeting the 1% criterion under the same original costs and constraints. Record what is included in the 5-second QPU component together with classical evaluation, communication and validation, so the scope of the comparison is explicit.</p>
<p>Under these assumptions, operational deployment is a No-Go, while bounded formulation and sampling validation is a Pivot. The exercise's illustrative Go criteria require the lower endpoint of a 95% confidence interval for returning a feasible plan within 30 seconds to reach 95%, a verifiable optimality gap ≤1%, supply-demand residual ≤10⁻⁶ MW and no output-bound violations. Record the classical upper and lower bounds that support the cost gap. Check for an end-to-end timing improvement at matching quality, including failures and timeouts in the return-rate denominator. These criteria and timings are proposed validation targets.</p>
