A state-preparation experiment with a fixed measurement budget spends the full budget even when the answer is easy to distinguish. Zecchin and colleagues' quantum sequential universal test, QSUT, uses past outcomes to choose subsequent measurements and stops when the evidence is sufficient. It distinguishes classes containing multiple possible states while controlling false rejection despite optional stopping. The error guarantee is theoretical; savings in state copies are evaluated through numerical experiments on single-qubit models.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

In the composite-null numerical example, the adaptive learned variational test (aLVT) reaches near-perfect detection power with fewer than 40 copies on average. At the maximum plotted budget of 100 copies, the block fixed-copy comparator still falls below aLVT's peak detection power. Separating the available budget from the average number actually consumed makes the early-stopping benefit visible. A misspecified measurement likelihood can also undermine the error guarantee. Applying the method to QPU verification therefore requires counting calibration, likelihood evaluation and state estimation alongside circuit execution.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-1">1</a>]</span>

S-RIPE, published the same day, exposes another measurement resource. Retaining pixel-level photon-number distributions alongside mean intensity improves localization of overlapping sources. Its experiment used five pseudo-thermal sources; sub-nanometre localization is a separate simulation-based prospect. This is an imaging result rather than a quantum-computing demonstration. Its connection to state-verification design is the choice of which information to preserve. A prospective review should compare the number of copies consumed and the statistics retained as distinct costs.<span class="citation">[<a href="#ref-2">2</a>]</span>
