# Using symmetry and conservation to test scientific computation

A spring with no friction or external energy input should keep oscillating with the same amplitude. Yet the amplitude computed by forward Euler grows with time. This review starts from that discrepancy to ask how symmetry and conservation laws can guide the design and testing of scientific computations.

Watch the kinetic and elastic energy bars for the exact reference motion. As one falls, the other rises, keeping their sum fixed. The two timelines beside them show the same motion with a different choice of time origin. The spring obeys the same law in either description. For this spring, action is the time integral of kinetic minus elastic energy. Its action is also unchanged by this shift. Noether's theorem connects that time-translation symmetry to conserved energy.

Whether a numerical calculation retains that relationship is a separate question. Normalize the spring’s position and velocity by fixed reference values. Plot each pair as one point in the phase plane. For this oscillator, states with the same energy lie on a circle. This is not the mass's path through space. Forward Euler takes a straight step along the tangent from the current state. The new point falls outside the circle, at a higher energy. With the time step used here, each update increases the energy by one percent.

The energy graph shows what happens when that small increase repeats. After two thousand equal steps, Euler's energy is about 440 million times its starting value. Velocity Verlet solves the same equation with the same time step, but its curve stays close to the initial energy. The enlarged lower panel shows an error within a quarter of one percent of that initial value throughout this run. The comparison tells us that the numerical method changes how energy error accumulates. A small energy error still does not guarantee correct oscillation timing.

When AI predicts motion, we also need to test the model it has learned. A Hamiltonian neural network learns an energy-like function. Previously, q and u were normalized position and velocity. Here q and p are the coordinate and canonical momentum. Hamilton’s equations use derivatives of the learned function to set how those two quantities change with time. In this construction, the learned function has no explicit time dependence. The resulting continuous-time motion conserves that learned function. Whether that function represents the actual energy still needs checking. So does the error introduced by step-by-step numerical integration.

The spring exposes a numerical error. The learning diagram explains why the learned law needs scrutiny too. Symmetry supplies constraints for a model, while conserved quantities such as energy help test the calculation. The review's central argument is to build those constraints into the design and then check whether the resulting predictions are physically sound. If work or heat passes into or out of the system, that exchange must also be included in the model.
